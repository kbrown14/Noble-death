# Women's Noble Death and Martyrdom in Antiquity: A Digital Corpus

A collection of ancient passages in which a woman's death is presented as
noble, exemplary, or otherwise significant for the moral, political, or
religious argument of its source. Each passage is given in the original Greek
or Latin with an English translation, an analytical note, thematic tags, and
comparative fields. The site can be browsed, searched in the original
languages, and explored through side-by-side comparison, a tag-similarity
network, and a timeline.

Edited and translated by Katie Brown, Doctoral Candidate in Religious Studies,
University of Texas at Austin. Version 1.0, October 2026. Corrections and
suggestions: brownk@utexas.edu.

## Purpose

The corpus accompanies research on women's noble death and martyrdom in
Second Temple Jewish, Roman, and early Christian antiquity and provides the
primary-text base for the comparative analysis in the editor's dissertation.

Material usually studied in separate fields (Maccabean martyr literature,
Roman *exempla* of female suicide, early Christian *passiones*) is gathered
here and described in the same terms, so that shared motifs, rhetorical
strategies, and structures of authority can be compared across traditions.

The site keeps three things apart: the ancient text, given from an identified
edition; the translation, which stays close to the source; and the editor's
analysis (notes, tags, and comparative fields), which is interpretation rather
than data supplied by the sources.

## Methodology

The Methodology tab on the site is the main statement of editorial
principles. In brief:

**Inclusion.** Each entry is a discrete ancient passage, not a modern
retelling. A passage qualifies when a woman's death (or, occasionally, the
deaths of a group of women) carries significance beyond the event: martyrdom,
moral exemplarity, political indictment, vindication of piety, resistance to
coercive authority, or something comparable. Historicity is not a criterion,
so legendary figures such as Polyxena and Lucretia appear alongside women in
historiographical narratives.

**Source and interpretation.** The Text section presents the ancient passage.
The analytical note is the editor's interpretation; where it paraphrases an
argument the source makes itself, it attributes the claim to the text. Tags
and the comparative fields are classifications made for this corpus.

**Tags.** A tag is assigned only where the passage's own language or action
supports it (an explicit comparison, a named object or event, a repeated
formulation, clearly represented conduct), not because a theme seems
adjacent. For display, tags are grouped into six categories in
`data/taxonomy.js`; the grouping affects display only. Thirteen tags
currently occur once, which reflects the size of the corpus rather than a
claim that the motif is unique.

**Comparative fields.** Authority challenged names the concrete ruler,
regime, office, or family structure involved rather than a broad abstraction.
Witnesses records who the narrative places at the death.

**Tag definitions.** Where tags could overlap, they are applied by these rules:

- *suicide*: she kills herself, by any means (including self-immolation).
- *voluntary death*: she willingly accepts a death inflicted by others;
  distinct from suicide.
- *witnessed death*: the narrative places others present at her death.
- *spectacle*: her death or body is publicly staged or displayed.
- *family death*: the deaths of family members occur in the same episode.
- *mother, wife, daughter*: her kinship role is stated in the passage;
  maternal virtue, spousal devotion, and filial devotion describe her conduct
  in that role, and maternal love the affection between mother and child.
- *immortality*: the passage voices hope of life after death or resurrection.
- *imitation of Christ*: the passage identifies her suffering with Christ's.
- *disputed tradition*: the passage itself signals uncertainty about how she
  died.
- *martyr narrative*: the passage narrates a death for refusing to abandon
  one's religion, Jewish or Christian.
- *well-born*: the passage states her respectable or high birth; it does not
  by itself imply aristocratic rank.
- *genre and context tags*: historiography, poetry, epistolary, rhetoric, and
  Second Temple Judaism follow each entry's Genre and Tradition fields.

**Compounded marginality.** This tag marks a woman whom the narrative places
in more than one subordinate position at once, so that her gender combines
with another status that further limits her protection or her voice: youth and
unmarried daughterhood under a father's authority, captivity, enslavement, or
freed status. It is applied only where the passage itself draws attention to
that combination, not on the basis of what is known about the woman from other
sources.

**Indictment and affirmation.** Many notes describe a death as working two
ways: diagnostically, exposing the failure of a ruler, regime, father, or the
men around her; and affirmatively, vindicating a value or community such as
chastity, *libertas*, Torah fidelity, or Christian confession.

**Translations.** Unless stated otherwise, translations are the editor's own,
made from the text printed beside them. They follow the source's syntax,
repeated vocabulary, and rhetorical structure closely, since those features
matter for comparison. Square brackets always mark the editor's words: a
summary of omitted material (which also appears in the original-language
text, so nothing is cut silently), or a word supplied for sense, as in "the
servant [of God]". Where the original is corrupt or disputed, superscript
numbers refer to notes at the end of the translation (at present only in
Pseudo-Philo).

**Editions.** Each entry names the edition of its original-language text,
any digital transcription used, and any departure from that edition (for
example, Ovid, *Met.* 13.471, where the corpus reads *non captiva* with most
modern editions and Magnus prints *nunc captiva*).

## Features

- **Texts:** all entries as cards, with keyword search over author, title,
  reference, and tags; filters by tradition, mode of death, genre, and tag;
  and sorting by date, author, or title.
- **Entry view:** reference, edition, analytical note, comparative fields
  (authority challenged, witnesses), grouped tags, the text
  in Translation, Original, or Parallel view, links to other versions of the
  same story, and a link to an online text.
- **Text Search:** searches the Greek and Latin directly, showing the number
  of matches per passage and up to three highlighted excerpts.
- **Compare:** up to five entries side by side on date, genre, mode of death,
  authority challenged, witnesses, and tags, with the tags they share
  and the tags unique to each.
- **Network:** passages linked by tag similarity, with a minimum-similarity
  slider, a same- or cross-tradition filter, and an option to hide versions
  of the same story.
- **Timeline:** passages plotted by date of composition or by narrative
  setting.
- **Links to entries:** every entry has a permanent address, such as
  `index.html#livy-lucretia`.

## How the tools work

### Text Search

Text Search is meant to show where a word occurs across the corpus,
whatever the conventions of the edition and whether or not the user can type
polytonic Greek. It searches a normalized copy of each text but displays
results in the original spelling.

Bracketed editorial material is removed before searching. Greek is
normalized by removing all diacritics (accents, breathings, diaeresis, iota
subscript), ignoring case, and treating final ς as σ, so that ψυχή, ψυχῇ,
and ΨΥΧΗ all match ψυχη. Because removing diacritics shortens the text, the
search keeps a map from each position in the normalized copy back to the
original, which lets matches be highlighted in the accented Greek.

Greek can also be searched in Latin letters. Each Greek word is
transliterated and compared with the query:

| Greek | Type | Greek | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| η | e | θ | th |
| ω | o | φ | ph |
| υ | u or y | χ | ch |
| ξ | x | ψ | ps |
| γ before γ, κ, ξ, χ | n (ἄγγελος, angelos) | rough breathing | h or nothing (ἱερός, hieros or ieros; ῥ, rh) |

Vowel length is not distinguished and macrons in the query are ignored. The
scheme is a search convenience, not a scholarly transliteration standard.

In Latin, case is ignored, and u/v and i/j count as the same letter, since
editions print them differently (*uirgo*, *virgo*).

The search matches word forms, not lemmas, so inflected forms are not grouped
under a headword. It also matches substrings: a short query will find
letters inside longer words, and a query in Greek script can run across a
word break. Other Latin spelling variation (*lachrymas*, *lacrimas*) is not
normalized.

### Network

The Network shows which passages, including passages from different
traditions, draw on similar thematic material. Each entry is treated as a set
of tags, and every pair of entries receives a Jaccard similarity score:

```
similarity(A, B) = shared tags / all distinct tags in A and B
```

The score runs from 0 (no tags shared) to 1 (identical tags). It measures
the proportion of overlap rather than a raw count, so two passages sharing
six of eight tags score higher than two sharing six of thirty, and an entry
with many tags does not link strongly to everything for that reason alone.

Of the 190 possible pairs, 182 share at least one tag. The strongest link is
between Livy and Ovid on Lucretia (0.79). At the default threshold of 6%, 162
links are shown. Line thickness is proportional to the score; hovering over a
line shows the score, and clicking it lists the shared tags.

The minimum-similarity slider (0 to 50%) hides weak links, most of which rest
on the commonest tags: resolve and witnessed death (14 entries each), courage
(12), suicide (11), and honor vs. shame (10). "Hide same-story parallels"
removes links between versions of the same story (entries with the same
`storyGroup`), so that likeness of plot does not obscure likeness across
stories. The tradition filter shows links within one tradition or only between
traditions.

Positions in the graph come from a force simulation that pulls strongly
linked passages together. Clusters are meaningful; exact position and
left-to-right order are not. All tags carry equal weight, and the scores are
only as reliable as the tagging.

### Timeline

The Timeline sets the date a text was written against the date of the story
it tells, since a legendary death may be narrated centuries later. Each entry
has a `compositionYear` and a `settingYear` (negative numbers are BCE), and
the toggle switches between them.

Most dates are estimates or traditional dates, so each point is approximate.
Hovering over a composition date shows the date as worded in the entry (for
example, "Composed c. 100–120 CE"); setting dates are shown with "c.". Ranges
are not drawn.

The axis is linear, with a margin of 4% of the date range (at least 20 years)
on each side. Tick intervals are chosen from 25, 50, 100, 200, 250, 500, or
1000 years to keep labels about 78 pixels apart. There is no year 0; the tick
there is labelled "1 CE". Labels are placed in date order on the lowest row
where they do not overlap another label, so the height of a point has no
significance.

## Textual verification

In November 2026 the original-language texts were compared word by word with
digital copies of their cited editions (Perseus Digital Library and Open Greek
and Latin for 18 passages; a scan of the 1527 edition for Pseudo-Philo), and
discrepancies were corrected. Still to be checked:

- Ovid, *Fasti*: the text follows Musisque Deoque, which could not be
  consulted. Seven readings that differ from Frazer (for example *tum*/*tunc*,
  *fereris*/*ferens*, *quoque*/*quove*) are unconfirmed.
- *Passio Perpetuae*: the only digital text of Robinson (1891) is
  uncorrected OCR. Three readings need checking in print: ch. 3
  *profectus*/*profecto*, ch. 20 *acu*, and ch. 21 *et* before *memor esto*.
- Pliny, *Ep.* 3.16: the print edition behind the Perseus text is not
  recorded.

## Technical notes

The site is plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript with no build step. It can be
opened directly from disk or served from any static host such as GitHub
Pages.

```
index.html          markup, styles, and application code
data/corpus.js      the corpus entries
data/taxonomy.js    tag display categories
README.md
```

The data files are ordinary scripts rather than `.json` files because
browsers do not allow `fetch()` from a page opened from disk. The value of
`CORPUS` is valid JSON.

The Network and Timeline use [D3](https://d3js.org/) 7.9.0, loaded from
cdnjs. Fonts (Cormorant Garamond, Source Sans 3, IBM Plex Mono) come from
Google Fonts; without a connection they fall back to system fonts. Colors and
fonts are defined once as CSS variables in `:root`. Tradition colors are set
in `TRADITION_COLORS` in `index.html`.

The navigation uses ARIA tab roles, the entry view is a modal dialog that
keeps keyboard focus and closes with Escape, and cards can be reached by
keyboard. The site requires a current browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or
Safari).

## Data format

Each entry in `data/corpus.js` has these fields:

| Field | Description |
|---|---|
| `id` | Lowercase, hyphenated identifier, used as the entry's web address. Do not change it once published. |
| `author`, `title`, `ref` | Author or source, short title, and ancient reference. |
| `textEdition` | Edition of the original text, digitization used, abridgements, and departures from the edition. |
| `originalLanguage` | `"Greek"` or `"Latin"`. |
| `originalText`, `translationEn` | The passage and its translation, each with the same bracketed omission notices. |
| `tradition` | `"Second Temple Jewish"`, `"Roman"`, or `"Early Christian"`. |
| `genre`, `deathMode` | Genre and mode of death. |
| `period`, `compositionYear` | Composition date in words, and as a year (negative for BCE). |
| `setting`, `settingYear` | Narrative setting in words, and as a year. |
| `summary` | The analytical note. |
| `tags` | List of analytical tags. |
| `authorityChallenged`, `witnesses` | Comparative fields. |
| `storyGroup` | Optional. Shared by versions of the same story (for example `"lucretia"`). |
| `sourceLink` | Optional. Link to an online text. |

Section, chapter, verse, and line numbers in the texts are set in small type
automatically when they appear as `[203]`, as `1.58` or `14.` at the start of
a line or after punctuation, on a line of their own, or before a Greek word.

To add an entry, add an object with all the fields above to `data/corpus.js`.
Reuse existing tags where possible, and add any new tag to a category in
`data/taxonomy.js` (otherwise it is listed under "Other"). Mark omissions in
both texts, name the edition, and set `storyGroup` if the story is already in
the corpus. Then open the entry by its address, try a search in the original
language, and check the Network, Timeline, and Compare views.
