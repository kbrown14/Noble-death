About this corpus

This digital corpus brings together ancient passages in which a woman's death is presented as noble, exemplary, or otherwise significant for the moral, political, or religious argument of the source. It spans Second Temple Jewish and Roman material from approximately the second century BCE to the early third century CE.

This corpus accompanies research on women's noble death and martyrdom in Jewish and Roman antiquity and provides the primary-text base for the comparative analysis developed in the editor's dissertation.

What qualifies for inclusion

Each entry represents a discrete ancient passage rather than a modern retelling. Included passages concern the death of a named or unnamed woman—or, in a small number of cases, a group of women—whose death carries significance beyond the event itself. Depending on the source, that significance may take the form of martyrdom, moral exemplarity, political indictment, vindication of piety, resistance to coercive authority, or another comparable function.

Inclusion does not depend on the historicity of the underlying event. Legendary and mythic figures such as Polyxena and Lucretia therefore appear alongside figures presented within historiographical narratives by authors such as Tacitus and Josephus. The corpus is concerned with how ancient texts construct and interpret meaningful death, not with adjudicating whether the narrated events occurred as described.

How to use this site

Texts browses the full corpus; Text Search searches the original Greek and Latin directly; Compare sets passages side by side; Network visualizes thematic overlap between passages; and Timeline arranges them by date. The Methodology tab explains the editorial and analytical choices behind the corpus—how tags, ratings, and translations were produced, and how the Network and Text Search tools work under the hood.

Methodology

This page documents how the corpus distinguishes source from interpretation, how its analytical categories are defined and applied, and how the Network and Text Search tools work.

Source description and analytical interpretation

The corpus distinguishes as clearly as possible between the ancient source and the analytical framework applied to it. The Text section, including its Original, Translation, and Parallel views, presents the ancient passage itself. The Analytical note offers the corpus editor's interpretation of the passage's significance and should be read as an analytical claim rather than as a statement of authorial intention.

The Key themes tags and the comparative fields—such as Agency, Authority challenged, and Witnesses—are likewise analytical classifications created for this corpus rather than categories supplied by the ancient texts themselves. Where an analytical note paraphrases an argument made explicitly by the source—for example, 4 Maccabees' presentation of endurance as the triumph of devout reason over passion—the wording identifies that claim as belonging to the ancient text. Other interpretive statements represent the corpus editor's synthesis.

Tags

Tags are analytical descriptors developed for this corpus rather than terms drawn from an external controlled vocabulary. They identify recurring themes, motifs, objects, rhetorical strategies, social roles, and forms of action that permit comparison across otherwise disparate passages.

A tag is assigned only where it is supported by the language or action of the passage itself—for example, through an explicit comparison, a named object or event, a repeated rhetorical formulation, or a clearly represented form of conduct. Tags are not assigned solely because a concept appears thematically adjacent to the passage.

Most tags occur in more than one entry so that they can function comparatively in the Texts filters and Network view. A small number remain unique to a single passage where the corpus currently contains no genuine parallel. Such one-off tags reflect the present scale of the corpus and may acquire additional instances as the collection expands.

Agency

Agency describes the degree to which a narrative presents the woman as directing, consenting to, resisting, negotiating, or rhetorically shaping the circumstances of her death. The values are comparative within this corpus rather than absolute judgments about historical agency.

A High rating indicates that the text presents the woman as substantially shaping the scene—for example, by choosing the means, timing, or terms of her death, explicitly accepting it, resisting attempts to prevent it, or determining how others are to respond. A Low rating indicates that the woman is presented primarily as the object of another person's or institution's decision, with little or no reported resistance, negotiation, or self-direction. Intermediate cases are marked Medium.

Where an ancient source itself expresses uncertainty about the circumstances of a death, the rating follows that uncertainty rather than attempting to resolve it.

Authority challenged

Authority challenged identifies the specific ruler, regime, office, familial structure, or other form of authority against which the passage positions the woman, or to which it subjects her. The category is intentionally concrete: it identifies, for example, a named emperor, a monarchy, a conquering power, or a father's vow rather than imposing broader abstractions such as "patriarchy" or "empire" where the source itself does not require them.

The field therefore records the immediate structure of authority that produces, constrains, or is challenged by the death as the passage narrates it.

Translations

Unless explicitly stated otherwise, the English translations in this corpus are original translations prepared directly from the Greek or Latin text displayed alongside them. They are not adapted from existing published translations.

The translations aim to remain close to the source in syntax, repeated vocabulary, rhetorical structure, and significant verbal patterning rather than to produce a freely literary English version. This approach is intended to preserve features of the original that are important for comparison across the corpus.

Texts and editions

Each entry gives its ancient source reference—author, work, and relevant book, chapter, section, or line numbers—and identifies the critical edition underlying the Greek or Latin text wherever that edition has been established for the entry.

Where available, entries also provide a link to an online full-text source or repository. These links are intended as points of access rather than substitutes for the bibliographical identification of the text itself.

Bracketed abridgement notices indicate places where intervening material has been omitted, usually because it constitutes a digression or narrative material not directly relevant to the subject of the entry. Such omissions are marked explicitly rather than silently excised.

How the Network view works

The Network view visualizes similarity between passages on the basis of their analytical tags. Each pair of entries is assigned a Jaccard similarity score: the number of tags shared by the two entries divided by the total number of distinct tags represented across the pair.

This gives greater weight to proportionate overlap than to the mere presence of a shared tag. Two entries sharing six of eight total tags therefore form a substantially stronger connection than two entries sharing one tag out of twenty. Edge thickness reflects this similarity score.

The minimum-similarity control, the same-tradition and cross-tradition filters, and the option to hide passages that represent versions of the same story are intended to keep the visualization analytically useful as the corpus expands. These controls also limit the distorting effect of highly recurrent tags such as courage, resolve, or honor vs. shame, which might otherwise produce connections between a large proportion of the corpus.

Text Search

Text Search searches the Greek and Latin forms contained in the original-language texts of the corpus, independently of the Texts search, which searches only metadata (author, title, reference, tags). Greek may be entered in Greek script or in a plain-ASCII Latin transliteration; accents and breathing marks are ignored in both cases, and results preserve the spelling and diacritics of the source text. The transliteration scheme is chosen for accessibility rather than formal precision (η/ω/υ all simplify to e/o/u, θ→th, φ→ph, χ→ch) and should be understood as a search convenience rather than a scholarly transliteration standard. Searches are form-based rather than lemmatized: morphologically related forms are not automatically grouped under a single dictionary lemma.
